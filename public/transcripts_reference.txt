Pongal – A Taste of Home and Togetherness

When I think about traditional South Indian food, one dish that immediately comes to my mind is Pongal. It is a simple, comforting dish made mainly with rice and moong dal. There are two popular varieties: Ven Pongal, which is savoury and made with ingredients like black pepper, cumin, and ghee, and Sakkarai Pongal, which is sweet and prepared with jaggery, cardamom, and ghee.

For me, Pongal is more than just a dish. It reminds me of home, family, and the comfort of eating something warm and familiar. I think we often realise the importance of home-cooked food only when we move away from home for college. Hostel life can be exciting, but between classes, assignments, exams, and a busy schedule, there are days when we really miss home. Sometimes, all we want is a simple meal prepared by our family, served with love, without having to worry about anything else.

During those moments, even a bowl of Pongal can bring comfort. The softness of the rice, the aroma of ghee, and the flavour of pepper and cumin make it feel like a warm hug in the form of food. When we eat it at a college canteen or a South Indian restaurant, it might remind us of breakfast at home, sitting with our family and talking about our day. The food may not taste exactly the same as our mother's or grandmother's cooking, but it can still bring back those beautiful memories.

Pongal is also an important part of everyday South Indian food culture. Ven Pongal is commonly eaten for breakfast with coconut chutney and sambar. Sakkarai Pongal is especially associated with the harvest festival of Pongal in Tamil Nadu. Families prepare it to express gratitude for the harvest, nature, farmers, and the Sun. The traditional practice of allowing the pot to boil over represents abundance and prosperity.

Different families prepare Pongal in different ways. Some prefer Ven Pongal soft and creamy, with plenty of ghee and pepper, while others make it thicker. Sakkarai Pongal can include cashews, raisins, and cardamom. Some families prepare it in a traditional pot during festivals, while others use modern kitchen utensils. These small differences give every family's recipe its own identity.

According to me, the true taste of Ven Pongal comes from its softness and the balance of flavours. It should be warm, mildly spiced, and full of the aroma of ghee. When served with fresh coconut chutney and hot sambar, it becomes a simple but satisfying meal. Chennai, Madurai, Thanjavur, and many other places in Tamil Nadu offer wonderful opportunities to enjoy traditional Pongal.

Personally, what I love most about Pongal is the emotional connection it creates. Living away from home teaches us that we sometimes miss the simplest things the most. A familiar dish can remind us of family, festivals, and mornings when we never had to think about where our next meal would come from.

Finally, I believe that traditional foods like Pongal should be preserved because they carry our culture, memories, and family traditions.

Because when we move away from home, food becomes more than something that fills our stomach. Sometimes, a simple bowl of Pongal can bring back the warmth of home and remind us that, no matter how far we go, a part of home always stays with us.

Medu Vada – A Taste of Home Away from Home

When we talk about South Indian food, one dish that immediately comes to my mind is Medu Vada. It is a traditional snack made mainly from urad dal, or black gram. The dal is soaked, ground into a thick batter, mixed with ingredients like ginger, green chillies, curry leaves, and black pepper, and then deep-fried until golden and crispy. It is usually served hot with coconut chutney and sambar.

For me, Medu Vada is more than just a food item. It reminds me of home, family, and the comfort of eating something familiar. I think food becomes even more special when we move away from home for college. Staying in a hostel, managing classes, assignments, and exams can sometimes feel tiring. And on certain days, we really miss home-cooked food and the little things we never appreciated enough before leaving home.

During those moments, even a simple plate of Medu Vada can bring a sense of comfort. When I see freshly fried vadas at a college canteen or a nearby South Indian restaurant, the smell and taste remind me of home. It may not be exactly like the food prepared by our family, but for a little while, it makes us feel connected to our roots. I believe many college students can relate to this feeling. Sometimes, we don't just crave a particular dish; we miss the people and memories associated with it.

Medu Vada is a popular part of South Indian food culture and is commonly eaten for breakfast or as an evening snack. It is often served during family gatherings, festivals, and religious occasions. Since urad dal is its main ingredient, it also provides protein, although the amount of oil absorbed during frying can vary.

There are small differences in how people prepare Medu Vada. Some families add more black pepper and curry leaves, while others prefer extra ginger or green chillies. In Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, and Kerala, it is enjoyed in different meals and with different accompaniments. But the basic idea remains the same: a crispy outside and a soft, fluffy centre.

According to me, a perfect Medu Vada should be freshly fried, crispy on the outside, soft inside, and full of flavour. The combination of hot vada, coconut chutney, and sambar is simply wonderful. Cities like Bengaluru, Chennai, Mysuru, and Madurai have many places where you can enjoy this dish, but sometimes even a small canteen near college can serve something that makes your day better.

Personally, what I love most about Medu Vada is how it connects food with emotions. College life teaches us many things, including how much we value the comfort of home once we start living away from it. A familiar taste can remind us of family breakfasts, conversations around the dining table, and the feeling of being cared for.

Finally, I believe that traditional foods like Medu Vada deserve to be preserved because they carry our culture, memories, and personal stories.

Because when we live away from home, food can become a little piece of home that we carry with us. And sometimes, one familiar bite is enough to make us feel connected to the people and places we miss.

Mysore Pak - When we talk about Indian sweets, there are so many options to choose from, but one sweet that holds a special place in my heart is Mysore Pak.

Mysore Pak is a traditional sweet that comes from Mysore, Karnataka. It is mainly made using three simple ingredients: gram flour, ghee, and sugar. But even though the ingredients are simple, making a really good Mysore Pak takes patience and the right technique. The ingredients are cooked together until they form that beautiful golden sweet we all know and love.

For me, Mysore Pak is more than just a sweet. It reminds me of festivals, family gatherings, and those little moments when someone brings home a box of sweets and everyone gathers around to have a piece. There is something special about sharing sweets with family. Even a small piece can make an ordinary day feel like a celebration.

Mysore Pak is not something people usually eat with every meal, but it is an important part of celebrations and special occasions. In many South Indian families, sweets are shared during festivals, weddings, religious occasions, and family functions. Mysore Pak is also commonly given to guests as a gesture of happiness and hospitality.

One beautiful thing about Mysore Pak is that there are different versions of it. The traditional Mysore Pak is often firm and slightly crumbly, with a rich flavour of ghee. The softer version, which many people call soft Mysore Pak, has a melt-in-your-mouth texture. Different sweet shops have their own methods, so the taste and texture can vary from one place to another.

According to me, the real beauty of Mysore Pak lies in its texture and aroma. A good Mysore Pak should have a rich ghee fragrance, the right amount of sweetness, and a smooth, buttery taste. Some versions are slightly crisp and crumbly, while others almost melt as soon as you take a bite. Personally, I love that rich flavour of ghee because it makes the sweet feel so traditional and satisfying.

When it comes to famous places, Mysore in Karnataka is the place most closely associated with this sweet. The city is known for its traditional sweet shops, and Mysore Pak is one of its most famous specialities. You can also find delicious versions in Bengaluru, Chennai, and many other South Indian cities. But honestly, every shop has its own taste, and sometimes the homemade version feels the most special.

What I personally love about Mysore Pak is its simplicity. It doesn't need fancy decorations or complicated ingredients. It is a sweet that reminds us that sometimes the simplest things can bring the greatest happiness.

And finally, I believe that traditional foods like Mysore Pak deserve to be preserved because they carry stories from one generation to another. When we share our favourite foods and the memories connected to them, we also preserve a part of our culture.

**Because a sweet like Mysore Pak is not just something we eat; it is a little piece of tradition that we can taste, share, and pass on to future generations.**

Dharwad Peda – A Sweet Reminder of Home

In many Indian families, sweets are also given to guests as a gesture of happiness and hospitality. Thekua - When we talk about traditional Indian sweets, one sweet that holds a very special place in the hearts of people from Bihar and Jharkhand is Thekua.

Thekua is a traditional sweet snack made mainly from wheat flour, jaggery or sugar, and ghee. Sometimes, people add grated coconut, fennel seeds, or cardamom to give it extra flavour. The dough is shaped into small pieces, often pressed into wooden moulds to create beautiful patterns, and then deep-fried until golden brown. It is crispy on the outside, slightly firm inside, and has a lovely sweetness that makes it different from many other Indian sweets.

For me, what makes Thekua special is the feeling of home and tradition that comes with it. I imagine a family kitchen filled with the aroma of freshly fried Thekua, with everyone waiting to taste the first batch. Someone is carefully preparing the dough, another person is shaping the pieces, and the whole process becomes a family activity. These little moments make food more meaningful because they bring people together and create memories that stay with us for years.

Thekua is not just a sweet that people eat occasionally; it is also deeply connected to religious traditions and festivals. It is especially important during Chhath Puja, one of the most significant festivals celebrated in Bihar, Jharkhand, and parts of eastern Uttar Pradesh. During this festival, Thekua is prepared as a sacred offering to the Sun God. Families prepare it with great care, following traditional methods and maintaining cleanliness throughout the process. After the offering, it is shared among family members and the community as prasad.

One beautiful thing about Thekua is that every family may have its own way of preparing it. Some people use jaggery for a deeper, richer sweetness, while others prefer sugar. Some add coconut and fennel seeds, while others keep the recipe simple. The shape and texture may also differ depending on the mould used and how long the Thekua is fried. These small differences make every family's recipe unique.

According to me, the true taste of Thekua lies in its simplicity. A good Thekua should be golden brown, crispy, and full of the flavour of jaggery and ghee. The aroma of fennel seeds makes it even more special. It should be sweet without being too sugary, and its firm texture allows it to be stored for several days without losing its taste and crispness, making it a traditional sweet that is both delicious and practical for families to prepare and share during festivals and special occasions.

Til ke laddu - When we talk about traditional Indian sweets, we often think of kaju katli, gulab jamun, or laddus made for festivals. But today, I want to talk about a simple sweet that carries a lot of warmth, tradition, and childhood memories: Til Ke Laddu.

Til Ke Laddu is a traditional Indian sweet made mainly from sesame seeds, also known as til, and jaggery. The sesame seeds are roasted until they release their lovely nutty aroma, then mixed with melted jaggery and shaped into small round balls while the mixture is still warm. Some families also add peanuts, coconut, or cardamom to give them a slightly different flavour.

For me, what makes Til Ke Laddu special is the feeling of winter and home that comes with it. I can imagine sitting in the kitchen while someone in the family carefully roasts sesame seeds, filling the whole house with their warm, nutty smell. There is always a little excitement when the laddus are ready, because everyone wants to taste them while they are still fresh. These simple moments are what make traditional food so memorable.

Til Ke Laddu is especially popular during winter because sesame seeds and jaggery are traditionally valued as warming foods. People enjoy them as a snack or after meals, and they are particularly associated with festivals such as Makar Sankranti and Lohri. They are also a source of energy, with sesame seeds providing healthy fats, protein, and minerals.

Culturally, Til Ke Laddu has a special connection with Makar Sankranti, which is celebrated in different ways across India. In Maharashtra, people exchange sweets made with sesame and jaggery and say, "Tilgul ghya, god god bola," which means, "Take this sweet and speak sweetly." This beautiful tradition reminds people to let go of bitterness and maintain warm relationships. In other regions, sesame and jaggery sweets are also shared during winter festivals and family gatherings.

One interesting thing about Til Ke Laddu is that its preparation changes from region to region and even from one household to another. Some people make them with whole sesame seeds, while others use crushed sesame seeds for a different texture. In some places, peanuts or puffed rice are added, while certain families prefer a simple combination of sesame and jaggery. Some laddus are crunchy, while others are softer and easier to bite into.

According to me, the true taste of Til Ke Laddu comes from the balance between the roasted sesame seeds and the jaggery. A good laddu should have a rich, nutty aroma, a natural sweetness, and a texture that is neither too hard nor too sticky. The roasted sesame seeds give it a lovely flavour, while the jaggery adds warmth and depth.

If someone wants to experience this traditional sweet, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan, and many other parts of India are wonderful places to explore it, especially during the winter festival season. You can find Til Ke Laddu in local sweet shops, traditional markets, and homes where families still prepare them using old recipes.

Personally, what I love most about Til Ke Laddu is its simplicity. It does not need expensive ingredients or fancy decoration. A few everyday ingredients come together to create something delicious and meaningful.

Finally, I believe that traditional foods like Til Ke Laddu should be preserved because they connect us to our festivals, our families, and the wisdom of earlier generations.

**Because sometimes, the smallest sweet carries the biggest message: stay connected, share happiness, and always make room for traditions in our lives.**

Daal baati churma - When I think about traditional Indian food, one dish that immediately comes to my mind is Dal Baati Churma. It is one of the most famous dishes of Rajasthan, and for me, it represents the warmth, simplicity, and richness of Rajasthani culture.

Dal Baati Churma is actually a combination of three different things. Dal is a mixture of lentils cooked with spices, baati is a round, firm wheat dough ball that is traditionally baked or roasted, and churma is a sweet dish made from crushed baati mixed with ghee and sugar or jaggery. When you put all three together, you get a wonderful combination of spicy, savoury, and sweet flavours.

For me, what makes this dish special is the feeling of togetherness associated with it. Imagine sitting with your family on a relaxed afternoon, with a big plate of hot baati, freshly prepared dal, and sweet churma on the side. Everyone is talking, sharing food, and enjoying the meal. These are the kinds of moments that make traditional food memorable. It is not just about filling your stomach; it is about creating memories with the people around you.

Dal Baati Churma is a filling meal and is often prepared for family gatherings, weddings, festivals, and special occasions. In Rajasthan, it is also enjoyed as part of traditional meals. The main ingredients include wheat flour, lentils such as moong, chana, or toor dal, ghee, and spices such as cumin, coriander, and red chilli. Churma adds sweetness and balances the spicy flavour of the dal.

Culturally, this dish is deeply connected to Rajasthan's food traditions. The region's dry climate and long-standing food customs helped shape meals that were filling and could be prepared using locally available ingredients. Over generations, Dal Baati Churma became a symbol of Rajasthani hospitality. Serving it to guests is a way of making them feel welcome and giving them a taste of the region's heritage.

One interesting thing about this dish is that its preparation changes from family to family and region to region. Some people prepare baati in a traditional oven or over a fire, while others bake it in a modern oven. Some versions have plain baati, while others include fillings. The dal may be simple or made with a mixture of lentil.
